Faster CI: make observing it a priority

2026-10-29 · Ben · Building note

CI on the CampfireFM backend used to take about 13 minutes from push to green. Now it takes about 8. Any agent could have found that cut in one run. What we wanted was a way to keep finding them: a skill that measures CI every day, a report page in Uberblick that keeps the numbers, and a short list of recommended actions we pick from. This note covers how that works, the change it led to and why 8 minutes is still too long.

Why the wait matters more with agents

Agents now write code faster than most teams can get it merged. In September Linear wrote that AI coding has made CI a bottleneck: "Agents have made it exponentially faster to ship code, but validating those changes hasn't quite kept up at the same rate." Their test suites almost quadrupled this year, and they reworked CI to keep a pull request's wait at about five minutes.

CircleCI's 2026 State of Software Delivery sees the same thing across its customers. For the median team, throughput on feature branches rose 15 percent in a year while throughput on the main branch fell 7 percent. In their words: "Writing code is no longer the constraint." Once agents write the code, CI is often what decides how fast a change reaches customers.

We feel this in our own CI. It's not only the agent runs queueing for a green check. It's that a hotfix simply takes too long to land. When something is broken for our users, every minute of CI sits between the fix and the people waiting for it.

When people write the code, a slow CI run is an annoyance. You push, switch to something else and look back later. When ub-agents write it, CI sits on the critical path of every pull request, more than once. The implementer waits for green before it hands off. The integrator waits again on the merged tree before it merges. A run that waits for CI doesn't do anything else in the meantime.

So the number we care about is wall time: from the first job starting to the last one finishing. That is the time a push waits. The minutes all jobs add up to matter for the bill, not for the wait. Five minutes on ten parallel jobs is faster than eight minutes on one.

Observe CI continuously, not once

Point any agent at a slow pipeline and it will find something to cut. That's one run. Then tests get added, dependencies change, a job starts to hang now and then, and nobody notices until a push takes 15 minutes again. The harder question is how to observe CI continuously, store what you learn and keep acting on it. Our answer has four parts:

  1. A skill collects the data and recommends actions. The ci-health skill reads the GitHub Actions runs on main, fills in every day since its last run, and judges again which changes would cut the wait most.
  2. The results live on a report page in Uberblick. The numbers are stored on the page as data, one record per day, and its charts are drawn from them. Reports with the numbers inside shows how a page like this works.
  3. Your usual coding agent runs it on a schedule. Claude Code, Codex or whatever you already use can run the skill as often as you like. We run it once a day.
  4. We read the report and pick. Once a week we open our CI Health Report and see what our loop identified as the main areas to improve. We discuss it briefly and apply what we're confident about.

The report page holds three things, all kept up to date by the skill:

  • Charts. The median wall time of green pushes and its slow tail (P95). The share of run attempts on main that end red, re-runs included. The number of tests and the ratio of test code to product code.
  • Incidents. A point where the median moved by at least a minute and 15 percent, with the change that caused it. When no merged change explains a step, the report leaves it out instead of guessing.
  • Recommended actions. Up to three changes that would cut the wait the most, ranked by the minutes a day they would save. Each card names the change, the evidence in numbers and dates, and the workflow or job it touches.

The actions are judged again on every run. A card stays while its evidence holds and drops out once a merged change addressed it or the data no longer shows it.

A card has a button that opens a Claude Code session to discuss the action before changing anything. Most of the time the discussion ends in a pull request or an issue. The session then strikes the card through and puts that pull request below it, so the next run knows someone is on it.

From 13 to 8 minutes on the CampfireFM backend

Uberblick exists to make the way we build our main product, CampfireFM, work better. The CampfireFM backend ran all its checks in one CI job, one after the other: lint, a migration check, a Playwright image for two browser tests, then almost 8,000 tests. From mid-September to 27 October the report's 7-day median stayed between 12 and 13.5 minutes.

Its top card had been there since 14 October: split the pytest step across two parallel runners. pytest alone took a median 9.78 minutes, and everything else in the job 3.35. The card estimated about 4.9 minutes saved per push, or about 42 minutes of waiting a day at 8.6 green pushes. Two smaller cards sat below it: run the two Playwright tests in their own job (about 1.2 minutes a push), and run lint and the migration check beside the tests (about 1 minute).

We talked the card through in our standup, and the pull request went further than the card. It splits pytest into two batches of 3,537 and 4,395 tests, moves lint into its own job beside them, and removes Playwright altogether, with the old pipeline it served, which nobody had used since September. One change covered the first card and most of the third (the migration check still runs in the first batch), and made the second unnecessary. It merged on 28 October.

The report recorded the step as an incident the same day. Comparing the eight green commits before the change with the eight after it, the median wall time fell from 13.34 to 7.84 minutes, 41 percent. At the 8.6 green pushes a day of the week before, that's about 47 minutes less waiting every day. Each test batch now takes 7 to 8 minutes, and lint about one. The runners do more work than before, 15.45 minutes per push on 28 October against about 13 the day before, but nobody waits for that part.

The chart plots the daily median rather than the 7-day one, so the drop shows on the day it happened. 28 October is the first full day on the new layout, so it is early evidence. The green runs since then, through the night of 29 October, have stayed between 6.9 and 8.6 minutes.

The same loop in Uberblick

Uberblick's own CI is heavier. Most of its wall time goes to browser tests in Chromium and WebKit, split into shards that run side by side. When the report started on 10 October, a push to main waited about 17 minutes. In its first week it recorded two steps down, each with its cause: splitting the browser jobs (#1669, 24 to 13 minutes) and running WebKit on the iPad viewport only (#1725, 13 to 6.4). By 20 October the 7-day median was 8.6 minutes. Since then its cards have led to two more changes.

A third browser shard. From 17 to 23 October one Chromium shard ended last on 70 percent of 123 green pushes, 1.73 minutes ahead of the next job. The card said to add a third shard per browser and put the saving at about 50 minutes of waiting a day. That became issue #2258 and pull request #2259, merged on 24 October. The report recorded the step as an incident: over the eight green commits on each side, the median wall time fell from 11.96 to 8.95 minutes. The runner minutes all jobs add up to rose by 4.6 percent, because each run now has two more jobs. That's the trade we wanted: a little more runner time for a shorter wait.

No more apt on every run. The WebKit jobs installed their system libraries with apt on every run. On 23 October that install held four pushes on main until they were cancelled at about 45 minutes, and added 24 to 33 minutes to four green ones. The median hardly noticed: the 7-day median stood at 9.35 minutes. The 7-day P95 shot up to 28.3. That's why the report charts the slow tail next to the median. A median alone would have told us everything was fine. Since #2243 both WebKit shards run in Playwright's image, pinned by digest, and their suites start within a minute of the job starting.

More than splitting tests

Splitting a suite across runners is the obvious move, and it's only one of them. This is what else cut our wait between 14 and 28 October. Not every item came from a card; some came from just looking at the numbers once they were in front of us.

  • Delete what nobody needs. On the CampfireFM backend, Playwright only served an old pipeline nobody had used since September. Removing it took the image restore and the browser server, about 1.2 minutes, off every push. In Uberblick we removed phone-only web tests (#1726), browser checks the unit tests already covered (#1730) and browser journeys that tested the same thing twice (#1736).
  • Cut setup. A job that installs its tools on every run pays for it on every push. Our WebKit jobs now start from a pinned Playwright image instead of installing system libraries with apt (#2243).
  • Run each check only where it matters. WebKit runs at iPad size only, and macOS runs only the suites that depend on the operating system (#1725).
  • Run independent checks side by side. Lint got its own job on the CampfireFM backend. In Uberblick the package suites moved to separate runners, and the required checks went from 265 to 155 seconds (#1780).
  • Balance by measured time. Our Chromium shards are split by how long each test file takes (#1776), and the CampfireFM batches are split from stored test durations.
  • Stop tests from waiting on the clock. Some of our tests sat out real timeouts meant for production. We gave them shorter waits (#2138), and since 28 October every test must finish within 60 seconds, with a duration report kept for every CI job (#2459).

Red runs cost more than slow ones

A slow run costs its minutes. A red run costs its minutes, then the time it takes to find out why, then a whole second run. If one push in five fails for no reason of its own, the real wait for green is a good deal longer than the median wall time, and the median never shows it. That's why the report puts the share of red runs on main, re-runs included, right beside wall time, and counts a job that fails or hangs on a change it has nothing to do with as waste, the same as a slow job.

The bigger cost is trust. When red often means flaky, people stop reading failures and press re-run. An agent has no hunch to go on: it takes the failure at its word and sets out to fix code that was never broken, or it gives up and hands the pull request back to a person. Either way a flaky test teaches everyone, human or agent, to ignore the one signal CI exists to give, and the next real regression hides in the noise.

So we treat an unexplained failure like a bug, not like the weather. Someone reads it. If the code is wrong, we fix the code; if the test is wrong, we fix the test. A test we can't fix right away goes into quarantine with an issue to fix or delete it. What we don't do is add retries: a retry makes the chart look better and leaves the problem exactly where it was.

Our favourite example: a release check looked for the digits 0600 anywhere in a log, and failed whenever a random UUID happened to contain them (fixed in #2243). The report puts a number on what such failures cost. From 17 to 23 October browser tests failed on 41 of 191 pushes to Uberblick's main, about 55 minutes of waiting a day. Two fixes, #2145 and #2130, brought that down to about 12 for a few days. It didn't hold: by 27 October 36 percent of the week's runs on main had ended red again, and that card is back at the top of the report.

Eight minutes is still too long

Eight minutes is better, but a push still waits too long. The report has already moved on. Its one card now says to rebalance the two pytest batches: on 28 October batch 2 ended last on 14 of 15 green pushes, a median 1.19 minutes after batch 1. The card puts the saving at about 0.6 minutes a push, and says itself that this rests on one day of evidence.

That alone won't take us far, and we don't know yet what will. Finding out is what the daily report is for. Uberblick's own CI has crept back to about 10 minutes on main, and one Chromium shard still sets the pace; the report's newest card says to rebalance the shards by measured file cost.

None of this needed a CI expert. It needed us to watch CI every day and take the numbers as seriously as any other part of the product. The ci-health skill does the watching for us, and it works on any GitHub repository, with or without Uberblick. Without Uberblick it prints a table instead of keeping a page.
