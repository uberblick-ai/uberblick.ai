Faster CI: make observing it a priority

2026-10-29 · Ben · Building note

CI on the CampfireFM backend used to take about 13 minutes from push to green. Now it takes about 8. Any agent could have found that cut once. What we wanted was a way to keep finding them: a skill that measures CI every day, a report page in Uberblick that keeps the numbers, and a short list of recommended actions we pick from.

Why the wait matters more with agents

Agents now write code faster than most teams can get it merged. In September Linear wrote that AI coding has made CI a bottleneck. CircleCI's 2026 State of Software Delivery found that for the median team, throughput on feature branches rose 15 percent in a year while throughput on the main branch fell 7 percent. In their words: "Writing code is no longer the constraint."

We feel it in our own CI. Our ub-agents wait for green before they hand off and again before they merge, and a run that waits does nothing else. Worse, a hotfix simply takes too long to land: every minute of CI sits between the fix and the people waiting for it.

So the number we care about is wall time: from the first job starting to the last one finishing, the time a push waits. Five minutes on ten parallel jobs beats eight minutes on one.

Observe CI continuously, not once

Point any agent at a slow pipeline and it will find something to cut. Then tests get added, a job starts to hang now and then, and nobody notices until a push takes 15 minutes again. The harder part is watching CI all the time. Our setup has four parts:

  1. A skill collects the data and recommends actions. The ci-health skill reads the GitHub Actions runs on main and fills in every day since its last run.
  2. The results live on a report page in Uberblick, one data record per day, with the charts drawn from them. Reports with the numbers inside shows how such a page works.
  3. Your usual coding agent runs it on a schedule. Claude Code, Codex or whatever you already use. We run it once a day.
  4. We read the report and pick. Once a week we look at what the loop found, discuss it briefly and apply what we're confident about.

The report charts the median wall time of green pushes, its slow tail (P95) and the share of runs on main that end red. It lists incidents, points where the median moved, with the change that caused them. And it keeps up to three recommended actions, ranked by the minutes of waiting a day they would save. A card stays while its evidence holds. Its button opens a Claude Code session to discuss the action before changing anything; when that ends in a pull request, the session strikes the card through.

From 13 to 8 minutes on the CampfireFM backend

Uberblick exists to make the way we build our main product, CampfireFM, work better. The CampfireFM backend ran all its checks in one CI job, one after the other: lint, a migration check, a Playwright image for two browser tests, then almost 8,000 tests. Since mid-September the 7-day median had stayed between 12 and 13.5 minutes.

Its top card had been there since 14 October: split the pytest step across two parallel runners. pytest took a median 9.78 minutes of the job's 13, and the card put the saving at about 4.9 minutes a push, some 42 minutes of waiting a day. Two smaller cards suggested moving Playwright and lint into jobs of their own.

We talked it through in our standup, and the pull request went further: two pytest batches of 3,537 and 4,395 tests, lint in its own job, and Playwright removed altogether, along with the old pipeline it served, unused since September. It merged on 28 October, and the report recorded the incident the same day: over the eight green commits on each side, the median wall time fell from 13.34 to 7.84 minutes, 41 percent, or about 47 minutes less waiting a day. The runners do more work than before, but nobody waits for that part.

The chart plots the daily median, so the drop shows on the day it happened. It's early evidence: the green runs since have stayed between 6.9 and 8.6 minutes.

The same loop in Uberblick

Uberblick's own CI is heavier, mostly browser tests in Chromium and WebKit, split into shards that run side by side. When the report started on 10 October, a push to main waited about 17 minutes. Splitting the browser jobs (#1669) and running WebKit on the iPad viewport only (#1725) brought the 7-day median to 8.6 minutes by 20 October.

A third browser shard. One Chromium shard ended last on 70 percent of green pushes, so a card said to add a third shard per browser (#2259). The median fell from 11.96 to 8.95 minutes, for 4.6 percent more runner minutes. That's the trade we want: a little more runner time for a shorter wait.

The slow tail. On 23 October the WebKit jobs' apt install stalled. It held four pushes on main until they were cancelled at about 45 minutes, and added 24 to 33 minutes to four green ones. The 7-day median stood at 9.35 minutes, while the P95 shot up to 28.3. A median alone would have told us everything was fine. Since #2243 WebKit runs in a pinned Playwright image instead.

More than splitting tests

Splitting a suite across runners is the obvious move. This is what else cut our wait:

  • Delete what nobody needs. Playwright on the CampfireFM backend. In Uberblick, phone-only web tests (#1726), browser checks the unit tests already covered (#1730) and browser journeys that tested the same thing twice (#1736).
  • Cut setup. Start from a pinned image instead of installing tools on every run (#2243).
  • Run each check only where it matters. WebKit at iPad size only, macOS only for the suites that depend on the operating system (#1725).
  • Run independent checks side by side. Lint in its own job. In Uberblick, package suites on separate runners took the required checks from 265 to 155 seconds (#1780).
  • Balance by measured time. Shards and batches are split by stored test durations (#1776).
  • Stop tests from waiting on the clock. Shorter waits instead of production timeouts (#2138), and since 28 October a 60-second limit per test (#2459).

Red runs cost more than slow ones

A slow run costs its minutes. A red run costs its minutes, then the time it takes to find out why, then a whole second run. If one push in five fails for no reason of its own, the real wait for green is much longer than the median shows. That's why the report puts the share of red runs right beside wall time, and counts a job that fails on a change it has nothing to do with as waste.

The bigger cost is trust. When red often means flaky, people stop reading failures and press re-run. An agent has no hunch to go on: it takes the failure at its word and sets out to fix code that was never broken, or it gives up and hands the pull request back to a person. Either way a flaky test teaches everyone, human or agent, to ignore the one signal CI exists to give, and the next real regression hides in the noise.

So we treat an unexplained failure like a bug, not like the weather. Someone reads it. If the code is wrong, we fix the code; if the test is wrong, we fix the test. A test we can't fix right away goes into quarantine with an issue to fix or delete it. What we don't do is add retries: a retry makes the chart look better and leaves the problem where it was.

Our favourite example: a release check looked for the digits 0600 anywhere in a log, and failed whenever a random UUID happened to contain them (#2243). From 17 to 23 October browser tests failed on 41 of 191 pushes to Uberblick's main, about 55 minutes of waiting a day. Two fixes (#2145, #2130) helped for a few days, but by 27 October 36 percent of the week's runs were red again, and that card is back at the top of the report.

Eight minutes is still too long

The report has already moved on. For the CampfireFM backend its next card says to rebalance the two pytest batches, worth about 0.6 minutes a push on one day of evidence. Uberblick has crept back to about 10 minutes. We don't know yet where the next big cut is; finding out is what the daily report is for.

None of this needed a CI expert. It needed us to watch CI every day and take the numbers as seriously as the product. The ci-health skill works on any GitHub repository, with or without Uberblick; without it, the skill prints a table instead of keeping a page. To keep the report in a workspace, start with Install.
